Relativity & Electromagnetism

In V2p13-6, Feynman explores the intimate relationship between electromagnetism and the special theory of relativity. He first points out that, in stating the Lorentz force F=q(E+v×B), we failed to specify a reference frame — in which frame is v measured? And in which frame do we define E and B? Feynman says: “It turns out that any inertial frame will do. We will also see that magnetism and electricity are not independent things—that they should always be taken together as one complete electromagnetic field. Although in the static case Maxwell’s equations separate into two distinct pairs, one pair for electricity and one pair for magnetism, with no apparent connection between the two fields, nevertheless, in nature itself there is a very intimate relationship between them that arises from the principle of relativity. Historically, the principle of relativity was discovered after Maxwell’s equations. It was, in fact, the study of electricity and magnetism which led ultimately to Einstein’s discovery of his principle of relativity. But let’s see what our knowledge of relativity would tell us about magnetic forces if we assume that the relativity principle is applicable—as it is—to electromagnetism.”

The first question to address is the impact of relativistic velocities on charge. We know that mass, length, and time change at relativistic velocities. So we ask: Do fast electrons and stationary electrons have the same charge? Feynman makes two compelling arguments that electric charge is invariant.

Firstly, heating an electrically neutral block of matter, such as copper, does not give it a non-zero charge. At higher temperatures, particles have greater thermal kinetic energy. Since the mass of a single electron is 117,000 times less than the mass of a copper nucleus, the velocities of copper’s negative charges change with temperature 340 times faster than those of its positive charges. If the unit charge changed with velocity, copper’s negative charges would change more than its positive charges, and a block of copper would acquire a net charge. Even a change of one part per billion would produce many tons of electrostatic force between two heated 1 kg copper blocks. No such effect has been observed.

Secondly, electron energies and velocities are also changed by various chemical reactions. Feynman says: “a straightforward calculation shows that even a very small dependence of charge on speed would give enormous fields f From the simplest chemical reactions. No such effect is observed.” While charge is relativistically invariant, charge density is not. This is because length along the direction of motion is not invariant. The Lorentz contraction (see Feynman Simplified 1C Chapter 26) states that an object that has length L in its own rest frame appears to have a shorter length L in a frame moving at velocity v in the same direction as L. The equation is: L = L √(1–v²/c²)

Magnetism as a Relativistic Effect Now, let’s see how relativity and electromagnetism work together.

Imagine a charge Q at a distance r from the center of an infinitely long, current-carrying wire. We will take the realistic view that current results from the flow of electrons, while the positive nuclei are stationary: v=0. For negative electrons moving to the right with velocity v, electric current J is flowing to the left. In a real conductor, not all electrons move; those that remain attached to their atoms do not contribute to current flow. For our purposes, we can ignore stationary electrons and neutral atoms, and consider only the conduction electrons and the positive ions they leave behind. We define the wire’s positive and negative charge densities as ρ_p and ρ_n, respectively.

To understand why, recall what we learned about special relativity in Feynman Simplified 1C, chapters 25 through 29. In the limiting case that the objects collide (distance becomes zero), in one frame, they must collide in every frame.

Linear Superposition The equations of magnetostatics are: ∇·B = 0 ∇×B = j / εc2. Both are clearly linear in both B and j. This means the principle of linear superposition applies to magnetostatics, as it does to all of electromagnetism. The B field produced by two constant currents is simply the vector sum of the fields produced by each current separately.

Right Hand Rule Feynman ends this lecture with a comment about the right hand rule in electromagnetism. There are two ways to define the polarity of a cross product; they correspond to using either one’s right or left hand. The choice is arbitrary in electromagnetism, because nature makes a distinction between left and right only in weak force interactions. (See Feynman Simplified 1D Chapter 49.) Yet, mathematicians and physicists are right-hand bigots, repeatedly instructing students to employ the right hand rule exclusively. (I, of course, am not a bigot; I even have a left-handed friend or two.) Indeed, if we all used the left hand rule exclusively, we would obtain exactly the same final results. Some quantities, such as B, would have the opposite polarity, but all real measurable quantities, such as the direction of particle motion, would be the same. For example, the force F exerted on a charge q by the magnetic field B of current j is: F = q v×B F = q v × (J × r) / (2πε c2 r2). A leftie would get the “wrong” sign for each cross product. But, in this case two “wrongs” do make a right: two polarity inversions result in the correct sign of F. The real measurable quantities here are j, v, r, and F, not B. More generally, this issue arises because there are two types of vectors: polar and axial. Polar vectors are the more familiar and include: position, velocity, force, and the electric field E. Axial vectors are mathematical constructs that are not directly observable; these include: angular momentum, torque, and the magnetic field B. All axial vectors are defined by a cross product, and the physical effect due to any axial vector involves another cross product. The polarity of physical observables is defined by nature: two wires pull together if they carry parallel currents and push apart if their currents are antiparallel. We are compelled to get the correct polarity of physical observables. The polarity of mathematical constructs is decided by human convention. The essential point is consistency. If you sometimes use the right hand rule and sometimes use the left hand rule, your answer will be Wrong in a fundamental way, not just contrary to an arbitrary convention. If you always use the left hand rule, you will get the correct answers for physical observables, but your instructor will still mark your answer Wrong.

The Vector Potential We have solved the equations of magnetostatics ∇•B = 0 ∇×B = j/c^2ε_0 in a number of special, symmetric conditions. We now wish to solve these equations in a more general way.

In electrostatics, we found a general analytic strategy: calculate the potential ø for a distribution of charges and employ E=–∇ø. We will now find a corresponding strategy for magnetostatics.

Vector Potential A In electrostatics, the electric field is proportional to the gradient of a scalar field, because the curl of E is always zero: ∇×E=0. Magnetic fields are different: their curls are non-zero, but their divergences are always zero. We learned in our study of vector algebra that any vector field with zero divergence can be represented as the curl of another vector field.

We therefore define the vector potential A such that: B = ∇×A In V2p14-1, Feynman writes this out in component notation: B_x = ∂A_z/∂y – ∂A_y/∂z B_y = ∂A_x/∂z – ∂A_z/∂x B_z = ∂A_y/∂x – ∂A_x/∂y Note that: ∇•B = ∇•(∇×A) = 0 Since the last step is true for any vector field A (see Chapter 2), this representation ensures B always has zero divergence.

The equation B = ∇×A defines the curl of A, but does not completely specify A itself. Indeed, for any scalar field ψ, adding ∇ψ to A does not change its curl, as shown below: B = ∇×(A+∇ψ) = ∇×A + ∇×(∇ψ) = ∇×A This is true because ∇×(∇ψ) = 0 for any ψ, as we found in Chapter 2.

We have a similar situation in electrostatics: E=–∇ø does not completely define ø. There we usually make ø unique by adding the condition ø(r=∞)=0. In magnetostatics, we will usually make A unique by adding the conditions ∇•A=0 and A(r=∞)=0. These added conditions are arbitrary, and in some situations other choices may be more convenient mathematically.

We explore the vector potential beginning with a simple example: a uniform magnetic field B in the +z-direction.

We can also derive a solution for the vector potential A for a uniform B field using Stokes’ theorem. It says the circulation of any vector field A around a closed curve Γ equals the flux of the curl of A through the surface S enclosed by Γ. The equation is: ∫_Γ A•ds = ∫_S (∇×A)•n da ∫_Γ A•ds = ∫_S (B)•n da ∫_Γ A•ds = πr^2 B From above, we know A is perpendicular to both B and r, which means it must be parallel to ds. And by symmetry, A•ds must have the same value at every point on the circle. Hence: 2πr A = πr^2 B A = r B / 2 This is the same result we obtained above. Feynman notes that in this example we calculated A from B, which is the opposite of the normal process. Just as we can calculate ø from a charge distribution and then calculate E from ø, we will usually calculate A from a current distribution and then calculate B from A, as we will do next.

Finding A from Current J The principle equation of magnetostatics is: ∇×B = j/c^2ε_0 Inserting the definition of A yields: ∇×(∇×A) = j/c^2ε_0 ∇(∇•A) – ∇^2A = j/c^2ε_0 Since we conventionally set the divergence of A equal to zero, this becomes: ∇^2A = – j/c^2ε_0 In component notation this is: ∇^2A_x = – j_x /c^2ε_0 ∇^2A_y = – j_y /c^2ε_0 ∇^2A_z = – j_z/c^2ε_0 These equations are identical to the electrostatic equation: ∇^2ø = – ρ/ε_0 As Feynman says in V2p14-4: “All we have learned about solving for potentials when ρ is known can be used for solving for each com ponent of A when j is known!” Adapting the general equation for ø found in Chapters 4 and 13, we obtain: A(r) = (1/4πεc2) ∫ j(σ) dV / |r–σ| 0 V Here, the integral runs over all points σ within volume V. To avoid mathematical pitfalls, this equation should be solved in rectilinear coordinates (x,y,z), rather than polar coordinates, or other systems in which the coordinate axes change direction. For magnetostatics, where Ď•j=0, this equation ensures that Ď•A=0.

The same techniques used to solve for electric potential ø are applicable to solving for vector potential A, from which we obtain B. As Feynman says: “It’s a little more complicated than electrostatics, but the same idea.” Actually, it can be significantly more complicated mathematically.

Let’s see how some simple physical situations can be addressed by analogy to electrostatics.

Consider an infinitely long solenoid of radius w, carrying current J, with n coils per unit length. As before, we assume the coils are parallel and infinitesimally thin, and that each coil lies in a plane perpendicular to the z-axis that runs through the solenoid’s center. (We are neglecting the coils' slight winding pitch.) The x- and y- axes are indicated in the cross-sectional image in Figure 15-3.

We define j to be the current density per unit area. Vector field j is circumferential and non-zero only on the solenoid’s surface. The magnitude of j equals the product of (coils per unit length) multiplied by (current per coil): j=nJ. We see from the figure that the components of j are: j (x,y,z) = – j sinθ j (x,y,z) = + j cosθ j(x,y,z) = 0 Our task is solving for vector potential A, and from A, solving for B. Let’s first solve for A outside the solenoid (r>w).

Using the electrostatic analogy, a solution for ø from charge density ρ is also a solution for A from current density j , with ρ=j /c2. In this case, we have surface charges and currents, so we will use the x x symbol σ for the surface charge density, with σ = -σ sinθ σ = j/c²

Feynman has a trick to solve this problem. In V2p14-5, he says our σ is the same as the net charge density from two solid, uniformly charged, opposite polarity, cylinders that are slightly displaced in the y-direction. Since the equations are the same, the solutions are the same, even if the physical situations are not. The proof of this claim is in the next section.

As we proved earlier, for a cylindrical wire of radius w, with charge λ per unit length and with r=√(x²+y²), the electric potential is: φ(x,y) = + (λ/2πε) ln(w/r)

Here, the wire’s axis is in the z-direction and its center is at x=y=0.

For one wire centered at (x,y) = (0,0) with charge density +λ and a second wire centered at (0,+Δy) with charge density –λ, the total potential is: φ* = φ(x,y) – φ(x,y–Δy)

Note that moving the second wire up is the same as moving the y-axis down. We’ve done this before.

φ = ∂φ/∂y Δy φ = (λ/2πε) {–∂[ln(r)]/∂y} Δy φ = – (λ/2πε) (1/r) (1/2r) (2y) Δy φ = – (λ/2πε) Δy y/r² We see that the sign is correct: the negative cylinder at y=+Δy leads to a negative potential for y>0.

In the prior chapter, we said B is zero outside an infinitely long solenoid. Let’s check that.

B = ∇×A B = ∂A/∂z – ∂A/∂z = 0 (x and y components)

B = ∂A/∂x – ∂A/∂x = 0 (y and z components)

We can check that our result is consistent with Stokes’ theorem. B equals the curl of A, which is circumferential. This means the path integral of B around a circle of radius r (r>w) centered on the solenoid equals 2πrA. By Stokes’ theorem this equals the flux of B through that circle, which is πw²B, where B is the magnetic field inside the solenoid. The result is: 2πrA = πw²B 2πr (K/r) = πw² nJ / c²ε K = w² nJ / 2c²ε and: A = w² nJ / 2c²εr Note that the circular path integral of A equals 2πK for all circles larger than the solenoid. Because the flux of B is entirely within the solenoid, all circles with larger radii enclose the same flux.

毕奥-萨伐尔方程

在通常的约定∇•A=0下，由电流分布j产生的矢量势A的一般方程为：

A(r) = (1/4πε₀c²) ∫ j(σ) dV / |r–σ|

对于电流在很细的导线中流动的情况，这个方程可以稍作简化。体积元dV变为S（导线的横截面积）与dσ（沿导线轴线的无穷小位移）的乘积。对于细导线和恒定电流，我们可以假设j在每个横截面S上是恒定的，并且平行于dσ。在这种情况下：

j(σ) dV = j S dσ = J dσ

这里，J是流过导线的总电流，等于其电流密度j乘以面积S。矢量势方程变为：

A(r) = (1/4πε₀c²) ∫ J dσ / |r–σ|

这里，积分变量是σ，即沿导线轴线的坐标。通常的叠加原理适用：几根载流导线的势是它们各自势的矢量和。

接下来，回忆在静电学中，φ由几乎相同的方程控制，并且E=–∇φ。在那里，我们可以对积分取梯度，或者将梯度移到积分号内。后者相当于对梯度取积分。数学上，这表示为：

φ(r) = (1/4πε₀) ∫ ρ(σ) dV / |r–σ| E(r) = –∇φ = –(1/4πε₀) ∫ ρ(σ) dV ∇(1/|r–σ|)

这里必须记住，我们只对r取梯度，而不是对σ。类似地， B(r) = ∇×A(r)

B(r) = (1/4πε₀c²) ∫ ∇×{j(σ) dV / |r–σ|}

同样，旋度只作用于r，而不是σ。被积函数的x分量是：

j_z ∂(1/|r–σ|)/∂y – j_y ∂(1/|r–σ|)/∂z = j_z (r_y –σ_y)/|r–σ|³ – j_y (r_z–σ_z)/|r–σ|³

这是以下矢量表达式的x分量： j × (r–σ) / |r–σ|³

y和z分量类似，因此得到： B(r) = (1/4πε₀c²) ∫ j × (r–σ) / |r–σ|³ dV

在j流过细导线的情况下，此方程变为： B(r) = (1/4πε₀c²) ∫ J dσ × (r–σ) / |r–σ|³

In such instruments, an electric current in a magnetic field turns an axle against a restraining force; the angle of rotation precisely determines the amount of current flowing through the coil.

Current Generators

Having found that electric currents produce magnetic fields, physicists tried for many years to use magnets to produce electric fields, but to no avail. Finally, in 1840, Michael Faraday discovered the essential requirement that many others had missed: changing magnetic fields produce electric fields; static fields do not.

A changing current in a wire or a moving magnet induces an electric field that can drive currents in other circuits. Feynman says induction leads to “an enormous range of wonderful phenomena.” We will describe qualitatively some of the effects in this chapter, and perform detailed analyses later.

The Lorentz force law, established half a century after Faraday’s discovery, provides a natural explanation of induced currents. If a magnet moves past a wire, or if a wire moves past a magnet, there is a relative velocity v between the magnet’s field B and the wire. A force F is exerted on the wire’s charged particles in accordance with: F=qv×B. If that force has a component parallel to the wire’s axis, a current is driven along the wire.

Feynman notes that in a changing magnetic field, charges may experience different forces in different parts of a circuit. The net current is determined by the net electromotive force (“emf”). Magnetic materials, such as iron, are described as being either hard or soft, depending on whether or not they can be permanently magnetized. In an external magnetic field, atomic magnetic moments become aligned. In hard materials, that alignment remains when the external field is removed, thus making a permanent magnet. In soft materials, atomic moments randomize when the external field is removed, and the material is no longer magnetic on a macroscopic scale. Soft iron in electromagnets make them easy to switch on, off, or reverse.

Our new principle is emf = ∂/∂t(flux): the emf in a circuit equals the rate of change of magnetic flux through that circuit. A less mathematical statement of this principle is: a changing magnetic field produces an electric field that induces a current in a conductor. Perhaps the most important practical device employing this effect is the transformer, an example of which is shown. Here two nearby coils on a common axis influence one another through their magnetic fields. The left coil is powered by an alternating voltage (“VAC”) source that alternates between positive and negative voltages. That source creates an alternating magnetic field represented by the array of horizontal lines encircled by the coils. That alternating field changes the flux through the right coil, creating an emf that drives an alternating electric current that lights a bulb.

There is no direct mechanical or electrical contact between the left and right coils. Nonetheless, power is transmitted between them. Note that the number of turns in the two coils is different: the left has 8 turns whereas the right has 4 turns. If the coils are properly positioned, the same magnetic flux passes through each turn in both coils, This means the total emf in the left coil is twice that in the right coil — the right coil produces half the voltage applied to the left coil.

By adjusting the number of turns in each coil, we can make the left/right voltage ratio anything we wish. This is the essence of a transformer. In a step-up transformer, the output coil has more turns, making the output voltage greater than the input voltage. In a step-down transformer, the output coil has fewer turns, making the output voltage less than the input voltage.

In V2p16-4, Feynman introduces the concept of self-inductance. The changing flux in the left coil of the transformer affects both coils, not just the right coil. The emf in the left coil due to the changing field in the left coil is said to be self-induced. The self-induced emf acts in opposition to the driving AC voltage, resisting change. It is in some sense akin to mechanical inertia. Each time the AC voltage ramps up, energy is required to build up the magnetic field. The self-induced emf in effect absorbs the energy needed to build the field.

When we stated our new principle, emf = ∂/∂t(flux), we weren’t specific about the emf polarity. This polarity is defined by Lenz’s rule: the emf always opposes the flux change. If flux changes because current J increases, the induced emf drives a current J that is opposite to J. If flux changes because current J decreases, the induced emf drives a current J in the same direction as J. In all cases, the emf acts to minimize change of both currents and fields.

The electromagnet shown provides an example of self-inductance. Here, an electromagnet, the big dark gray “C”, is activated by a coil whose current can be switched on and off. If the switch has been closed ("on") for some time, the coil current and magnetic field will be stable at their maximum values.

If the switch is then opened, the coil current and magnetic field drop rapidly. The flux change in the coil creates an emf that strives to maintain a constant coil current and magnetic field. Large electromagnets have enormous self-inductances that create enormous emfs. Without a bulb or similar device to close the circuit, such powerful emfs create their own return paths, often causing dangerous and damaging arcs across an open switch or between coil windings. Feynman says: "The high voltage that appears might also damage … you, if you are the person who opens the switch!"

The bulb provides a safe path for the emf-driven current as it gradually decreases to zero. When the switch is opened, the bulb shines brilliantly, and then gradually fades.

Motors and sensitive electrical instruments often employ an electric current in a magnetic field: to deliver mechanical power, or to precisely measure current flow.

The electromotive force ("emf") in a circuit is defined as the integral along the entire circuit of the tangential electromagnetic force per unit charge. Feynman says: "emfs can be generated in a wire in three different ways: by moving the wire, by moving a magnet near the wire, or by a changing current in a nearby wire."

Chapter 18 Exploring Induction

In V2p17-1, Feynman says: "In the last chapter we described many phenomena which show that the effects of induction are quite complicated and interesting. Now we want to discuss the fundamental principles which govern these effects."

All induction phenomena are governed by two fundamental equations that are always valid: The Lorentz force: F = q(E + v×B)

Maxwell’s equation #2: Ď×E = – ∂B/∂t In the prior chapter, we introduced emf and the associated flux rule. These are shortcuts that can simplify analysis, but which involve assumptions that are not valid in all cases. We explore here when and how to properly use these shortcuts.

The definition of emf and the flux rule for emfs in electrical circuits are: Definition: emf = + ∫ ds•F/q Flux Rule: emf = – d/dt ∫ B•n da The first equation says: the emf in circuit Γ (a closed loop) equals the integral around Γ of the tangential component of the electromagnetic force per unit charge (F/q=E+v×B).

The second equation says: the emf in circuit Γ equals minus the rate of change of the magnetic flux through the area S enclosed by Γ. Here, da is the infinitesimal area being integrated over, and n is the unit normal to da. You will often see n da replaced by da; the latter is a vector with magnitude da and direction parallel to n.

Feynman isn’t explicit or entirely consistent about emf polarities, only saying that emfs always oppose flux changes. I shall strive to provide the most commonly used polarity conventions where possible.

Note that magnetic flux may change due to changes within a circuit, motion of the circuit as a whole, changes in the magnetic field, or any combination thereof.

Let’s first consider a simple case: a circuit of a changing size situated in a constant magnetic field. Figure 18-1 shows a rectangular circuit formed by a stationary U-shaped wire and a movable crossbar (thick black line). The crossbar moves upward at velocity v, while remaining perfectly horizontal. The circuit formed by the crossbar and three sides of the U is rectangular with width w and length L.

Figure 18-1 A Changing Rectangular Circuit The black dots in the figure indicate the constant magnetic field coming out of the screen.

Let’s calculate the circuit’s emf from the integral of F/q=v×B. The charges in the crossbar move upward at velocity v, while all other charges are stationary. The integral thus reduces to the sum along the crossbar. Vectors v and B are orthogonal and v×B points to the right, which is anti-parallel to ds. (The right hand rule specifies counterclockwise line integrals.) The integral is then: emf = ∫ ds•F/q = – w v B Note that we consider only the forces exerted on free charges, the current carriers in conductors. In a normal conductor, some electrons are not attached to specific atoms but move freely carrying electric current. Conductors also contain ions, with the opposite charge that experience the opposite Lorentz force, but which cannot move because they are secured in place by neighboring atoms. The securing forces cancel the Lorentz forces on the ions.

Now let’s compare the above result with the emf calculated from a changing flux. At each moment, the flux through the circuit equals BwL, field B multiplied by the enclosed area wL. Since L changes as the crossbar moves upward, the flux changes, resulting in an emf equal to: emf = – d/dt (flux) = – d/dt (wLB)

emf = – w B dL/dt = – w B v This is the same result obtained from integrating the electromagnetic force per unit charge – the flux rule and the emf definition agree.

This emf drives a current j that flows clockwise around the rectangle, as indicated by the arrows in Figure 18-1. Feynman simplifies the problem by assuming the circuit is made of high-resistance wire, making j small enough to neglect its effects, including the magnetic field it produces. Since j flows clockwise in this case, its polarity is negative a According to the right hand rule, and it generates a magnetic field into the screen that opposes the external field B. Our result can be generalized to circuits of any shape. As we have seen several times before, any closed loop can be subdivided into numerous small rectangles. Our above proof applies separately to each small rectangle. By the principle of linear superposition, when we sum over all small rectangles, we find that the total emf and total flux change are governed by these same linear equations.

We next consider another source of flux change: a stationary loop in a changing magnetic field. Again, let the loop be rectangular, with width w and length L. The emf from the flux rule is: emf = – d/dt (flux) = – d/dt (wLB)

emf = – w L dB/dt

We wish to compare this with the emf from the integral of electromagnetic force – but where is the force? For a stationary circuit, the velocities of all charges are zero and the force equation F/q=E+v×B reduces to F/q=E. It seems that the magnetic field has no effect. But it actually does. One of the seminal moments in the understanding of electromagnetism was Faraday’s experimental discovery that changing magnetic fields produce electric fields. Maxwell developed the equation for this effect, which we list as his second law: Ď × E = – ∂B/∂t

To honor his profound discovery, this is called Faraday’s law. Let’s recast this law. Recall Stokes’ theorem for any vector field β: ∫ β•ds = ∫ (Ď×β)•n da Γ S

As usual, Γ is a closed loop enclosing surface S, and n is the unit normal to infinitesimal area da within S. With Stokes’ theorem, Faraday’s law becomes: ∫ E•ds = ∫ (–∂B/∂t)•n da Γ S ∫ E•ds = – ∂/∂t (flux of B through S)

When v=0 for all charges, the Lorentz force becomes F/q=E, yielding: ∫ ds•F/q = – ∂/∂t (flux of B through S)

emf = – ∂/∂t (flux)

We again recover the flux rule. Thus emfs are created by both moving charges and changing fields. Feynman notes that two quite distinct phenomena yield the same outcome, and that the flux rule applies equally to both. In V2p17-2, Feynman says: “We know of no other place in physics where such a simple and accurate general principle requires for its real understanding an analysis in terms of two different phenomena. Usually such a beautiful generalization is found to stem from a single deep underlying principle. Nevertheless, in this case there does not appear to be any such profound implication. We have to understand the ‘rule’ as the combined effects of two quite separate phenomena.

“We must look at the ‘flux rule’ in the following way. In general, the force per unit charge is F/q=E+v×B. In moving wires there is the force from the second term. Also, there is an E field if there is somewhere a changing magnetic field. They are independent effects, but the emf around the loop of wire is always equal to the rate of change of magnetic flux through it.”

Feynman stresses an important difference between the two emf-producing phenomena. The v×B term of emf requires matter: particles that have both q and v. Most commonly, that matter will be a physical wire, but a beam of charged particles also suffices. But the E term of emf can exist without matter. Wherever B changes, even in completely empty space, it creates an E field. Even an imaginary loop in empty space that encloses a changing magnetic flux will have a non-zero curl of E, a non-zero integral of E•ds. This is quite different from the E fields from static electric charges that always have zero curl, Ď×E=0.

In the first case, an emf exists but there is no flux change. Figure 18-2 shows a disk spinning on an axle, and exposed on one side to a constant magnetic field. Let the disk and its axle be made of a non-magnetic, electrical conductor, such as copper.

The circuit does not move, and the magnetic field does not change; hence the flux does not change. Yet, there is an emf. In the second case, the flux change is large but the emf is much smaller. Figure 18-3 shows two images, each with a galvanometer connected to two long rectangular conductors with a small conducting bar (black square) that electrically connects the rectangles at one spot.

Let’s now compare this to the emf from the integral of F/q. The emf in a circuit never comes from charges flowing in that current. The velocity u of current-carrying charges is always parallel to the direction of the circuit path ds, therefore u×B is orthogonal to ds and (u×B)•ds is zero. The emf in this circuit comes entirely from free charges in the sliding bar. emf = – ∫ (v×B)•ds = – v B ∫ds = – v B w₁.

By our construction w₁ >> w₂, so the flux change has a much greater magnitude than does the emf. This is the key point: the flux rule works when there is no change in the material of which the circuit is comprised – the actual atoms through which current flows must remain the same. The flux rule may not work if the circuit changes by redirecting current through different atoms.

In the spinning disk in a static magnetic field (see Figure 18-2), current flows through the disk at the same coordinate positions but through a continually changing group of atoms. This is why there is an emf despite no flux change, contrary to the flux rule. As the bar slides in a static magnetic field (see Figure 18-3), current flows through different atoms in the rectangular conductors. This is why there is a large flux change with a much smaller emf, again contrary to flux rule.

We say the flux may not work if the circuit material changes, but it might work in special circumstances. If we change the sliding bar example so that w₁ = w₂, we return to the situation of Figure 18-1, the U-shaped wire with a sliding crossbar, which we found does obey the flux rule.

When in doubt, go back to Maxwell’s equations and the Lorentz force, which are always valid.

An Apparent Paradox

In V2p17-5, Feynman presents an apparent paradox, saying: “A paradox is a situation which gives one answer when analyzed one way, and a different answer when analyzed another way, so that we are left in somewhat of a quandary as to actually what should happen. Of course, in physics there are never any real paradoxes because there is only one correct answer; at least we believe that nature will act in only one way (and that is the right way, naturally). So in physics a paradox is only a confusion in our own understanding.”

The apparent paradox is illustrated in Figure 18-6. The axle of a non-conducting disk pivots on frictionless bearings (not shown). Attached to the disk are six separated conducting balls, and a loop of superconducting wire carrying current j.

Figure 18-6 Disk, Balls & Current Loop

The disk is initially stationary. As long as the superconducting loop (central black circle) remains cold enough, it has zero electrical resistance, and the current j will flow inside it forever. The circulating current creates a magnetic field pointing upward.

But as the superconductor warms, its temperature eventually exceeds the limit for superconductivity. The current j then drops rapidly to zero, and the magnetic flux drops to zero as well. The flux change induces a circumferential electric field with E everywhere parallel to the original current j, because the induced emf seeks to maintain the prior B field by opposing its reduction.

Our first analysis is: the induced emf applies an azimuthal force to the conducting charges in each of the six balls. These forces exert six torques in the same direction that spin the disk counterclockwise, the same direction as the original current j.

Our second analysis is: by the principle of conservation of angular momentum, the total angular momentum must be the same before and after current j stops. Since the disk and all its components were stationary initially, they cannot be spinning after the current stops.

Our two analyses are antithetical – one must be wrong - hence the apparent paradox.

Feynman leaves this as a puzzle for you to solve. He says the solution “does not depend on any non-essential feature… the solution is not easy, nor is it a trick …When you figure it out, you will have discovered an important principle of electromagnetism.” You can check your answer in the section at the end of this chapter.

AC Current Generator

We begin with an alternating-current generator. Figure 18-7 shows a conducting loop mounted on a conducting axle, with a uniform, constant magnetic field B that points out of the screen (as represented by the dots).

Let θ be the coil rotation angle. The flux through the coil at angle θ is given by: flux = B S cosθ

Here, S is the surface area enclosed by the coil, and θ=0 is the orientation illustrated in the figure, which is the orientation of maximum flux. If the coil rotates at a constant angular velocity ω (θ=ωt), the emf in the coil is: emf = – ∂(flux)/∂t = + B S ω sin(ωt)

Feynman says we can equate this emf with an electric potential, voltage V, provided that we do so in a region where ∂B/∂t=0 and therefore ∇×E=0. Our current equation for the electric potential is not well defined when the curl of E is non-zero. Since that is not an issue in this case, we can define V and V by: V = B S ω sin(ωt) = V sin(ωt)

The alternating voltage V is what most of us have in our home wiring. The current j flowing through the load resistor R is: j = V / R = V sin(ωt) / R

This current j is called an alternating current (AC), since it alternates between positive and negative values.

In Feynman adds this comment: “Since there is an electric field between the wires [coming from the coil], they must be electrically charged. It is clear that the emf of the generator [the spinning coil] has pushed some excess charges out to the wire until the electric field from them is strong enough to exactly counterbalance the induction force. Now let’s examine the energy delivered by the generator and the energy required to spin the coil.

Let n be the number of unit charge carriers per unit length of wire, and let v be the velocity and F be the force on each carrier. The power required to drive this current in an infinitesimal length of wire ds is: power = n ds F•v

This is: (the number of unit charge carriers in ds) multiplied by (the power exerted per unit charge). Recall that work equals F•x, the dot product of force and distance moved, and that power is the time derivative of work (F•dx/dt=F•v). In any wire, v must be along the wire parallel to ds, hence we can rewrite the prior equation as: power = nv F•ds

Current j is simply nv, the number of unit charges multiplied by their velocity. Also, the integral throughout the circuit of F•ds equals the emf. We therefore have: electrical power = j • emf

This result is evident even without all that math: electrical power is always the driving voltage (emf) multiplied by the current (j). Feynman just wanted to provide us some mental exercise.

Now let’s calculate the mechanical power required to rotate the coil, which is the source of the electrical power delivered to the circuit. In Chapter 15, we found that the magnetic moment µ of a coil equals the current j flowing through it multiplied by the area S that it encloses. The mechanical potential energy U of magnetic dipole moment µ in field B is: U = – µ•B = – (jS) B cosθ

The torque τ required to rotate the coil is the derivative of U with respect to angle θ is: τ = ∂U/∂θ. This yields: τ = j S B sinθ

The mechanical power required to drive torque τ at angular velocity ω equals τω, hence: mechanical power = ω j S B sinθ

Recall that: emf = ω S B sinθ electrical power = j • emf

We see that the electrical power output exactly equals the mechanical power input (energy is conserved once again).

Self-Inductance We assumed above that loop #2 had a current driven by an external voltage source and loop #1 had an induced current driven by the magnetic field from loop #2. We did not address what the field from the current in loop #1 does to loop #2. Clearly, there can be currents in both loops. The flux linking the two loops would then be the sum of each flux, since magnetic fields are governed by the principle of linear superposition. In this general case, the emfs in the two loops can be written: emf₁ = – M₁₁ ∂j₁/∂t – M₁₂ ∂j₂/∂t emf₂ = – M₂₁ ∂j₁/∂t – M₂₂ ∂j₂/∂t We see that each emf is the sum of two terms, each proportional to the current in one loop. The quantities M₁₁ and M₂₂ are called self-inductances and are commonly written: L₁ = M₁₁ L₂ = M₂₂ In the most common convention, these are positive quantities. All current loops have self-inductance, even those completely isolated from other currents.

For a single circuit around a closed loop, the emf due to a change in its own current j is: emf = – L dj/dt The emf opposes the change of current, and is often called a back emf. This is akin to mechanical inertia. In a circuit with self-inductance, energy is required to change its current, the rate of charge flow, just as energy is required to change a massive object's velocity.

Simplifying Maxwell’s Equations

In V2p18-9, Feynman says solving Maxwell’s equations is simplified by rewriting them in terms of the electric potential φ and the vector potential A. Recall that any vector field whose divergence is zero, as in ∇·B=0, must be the curl of some other vector field. This motivated us to define the vector potential A as: B = ∇×A. Recall also that this equation doesn’t fully define A, but we will get to that later.

Let’s now combine this with Faraday’s law: ∇×E = – ∂B/∂t = – ∂/∂t (∇×A). Since partial derivatives commute, we rewrite this as: ∇×E = – ∇×(∂A/∂t). Then ∇×(E+∂A/∂t) = 0. This means the vector field (E+∂A/∂t) always has zero curl. Thus it must equal the gradient of some other vector field that we define to be –φ, yielding: E + ∂A/∂t = – ∇φ, so E = – ∇φ – ∂A/∂t. Note this is consistent with the definition of φ as the electrostatic potential, because in statics ∂A/∂t=0. As we are moving beyond statics, we will now call φ the scalar potential.

We next substitute this expression for E into Maxwell’s first equation: ρ/ε = ∇·E = ∇·(–∇φ –∂A/∂t). Then ρ/ε = – ∇²φ – ∂/∂t (∇·A).

Finally, we substitute our expressions for E and B into Maxwell’s fourth equation: j/ε = c² ∇×B – ∂E/∂t. So j/ε = c² ∇×(∇×A) – ∂/∂t (–∇φ –∂A/∂t). We expand the last term and restate the first term using an identity from vector algebra: ∇×(∇×A) = ∇(∇·A) – ∇²A. Thus j/ε = c²∇(∇·A) –c²∇²A + ∂∇φ/∂t +∂²A/∂t². Then j/ε = ∇ (c²∇·A+∂φ/∂t) –c²∇²A +∂²A/∂t².

Be patient; it’s always darkest before the dawn. Now recall that A is not completely defined by B=∇×A. The potentials A and φ are mathematical constructs that are not measurable. We are free to add anything to these potentials that does not change what is measurable: the fields E and B. In particular, we can set the divergence of A equal to anything without changing B. Choosing ∇·A is called choosing the gauge. Feynman chooses the Lorentz gauge, defined by: c²∇·A = – ∂φ/∂t. This reduces the prior equation to: j/ε = – c²∇²A + ∂²A/∂t².

The Lorentz gauge also changes the charge density equation to: ρ/ε = – ∇²φ – ∂/∂t (∇·A). Substituting the gauge condition gives: ρ/ε = – ∇²φ + ∂/∂t (∂φ/∂t) / c². Rearranging the two source equations brings them into an identical form: ∇²φ – ∂²φ/∂t² / c² = – ρ/ε and ∇²A –∂²A/∂t² / c² = – j/εc².

This operator combination occurs so frequently in physics that it is symbolized by ☐ and is called the D’Alembert operator. ☐ = ∇² – ∂²/∂t² / c². So ☐φ = – ρ/ε and ☐A = – j/εc². Any equation of the form: ☐G = 0 is solved by a wave G propagating at velocity c. Hence this is called the wave equation. Waves propagating with another velocity v, such as sound waves, are governed by the same equation with c replaced by v. Indeed, in empty space where ρ=j=0, these equations tell us that φ and A are waves propagating at the speed of light.

Feynman ends this lecture saying: “We have been able to write the field equations in terms of A and φ in a form that is simple and that makes immediately apparent that there are electromagnetic waves. For many practical purposes, it will still be convenient to use the original equations in terms of E and B. But they are on the other side of the mountain we have already climbed. Now we are ready to cross over to the other side of the peak. Things will look different—we are ready for some new and beautiful views.”

Spherical Waves As stated earlier, all three-dimensional electromagnetic waves are superpositions of plane waves, perhaps a bewildering mix of different amplitudes, polarizations, and directions. If such waves happen to be spherically symmetric, they have important additional characteristics that we can describe.

A spherically symmetric wave is a function only of time and radial distance r from an origin O. Such a wave does not vary with either polar or azimuthal angles.

Feynman also notes that a collapsing wave g(r+ct)/r, a wave that initially fills an enormous space and subsequently collapses to a point, is also a mathematically valid solution of the spherical wave equation.

However, we do not include collapsing waves, because they are physically unrealistic. An expanding spherical wave can be created by energy radiated from a small object. But no known physical process emits energy at infinity that uniformly implodes. If someone discovers that our universe is enclosed in a spherical mirror that reflects radiation, I will have to rewrite this section.

Feynman addresses one additional point in this lecture: our spherical wave ψ has infinite amplitude at r=0. We have seen before that physical laws often have troubles with infinities and with zeros. Perhaps neither actually exists in nature.

The reason that our calculated ψ(r,t) makes no sense at r=0 is that a wave emitted at r=0 is inconsistent with the assumption of entirely empty space. If all space were completely empty, devoid of all charges, no electromagnetic fields would be emitted anywhere. What our analysis implicitly assumed was an active electromagnetic source of negligible size at origin O, surrounded by completely empty space. This assumption is a good approximation to an excited atom or an unstable particle. Hence our analysis is mathematically valid and physically realistic everywhere except at the source, where the empty space equations do not apply.

In V2p20-3, Feynman’s intuition finds a path toward solving the sourced-wave equation.

He says that for very small r, the retardation delay becomes negligible (r/c<<t), and in the limit that r goes to zero, ψ becomes: for r<<ct: ψ(r,t) = f(t) / r. This ψ has the same form as the static Coulomb potential. The potential Φ for a localized charge density ρ is given by: Φ = (1/4πε₀r) ∫ ρ dV. We know this potential satisfies the electrostatic equation: ∇²Φ = – ρ / ε₀.

Following this logic, Feynman says we may expect that, for very small r, ψ has the form: ∇²ψ = – S. ψ = f/r = ∫ S dV /4π. Now, we add the freedom for S to vary over time. At very small r, Feynman says time derivatives of ψ become negligible compared with spatial derivatives of ψ. This is because ψ=f/r, and at very small r any change of spatial coordinates makes an enormous change in ψ. Thus the spatial derivatives increase without limit while the time derivatives are unchanged. This means: ∇²ψ – ∂²ψ/∂t² /c² = –S(t) becomes: ∇²ψ(r,t) = –S(t).

The result of all this, Feynman says, is that the solution ψ to the sourced wave equation is: ∇²ψ – ∂²ψ/∂t² /c² = –S(t); ψ(r,t) = ∫ S(t–r/c) dV / 4πr. The only effect of the ∂²ψ/∂t² term is to retard time, substituting t with t–r/c. This analysis amounts to a suggestive argument rather than a compelling proof.

Waves from an Extended Source Using the principle of linear superposition, we can obtain the solution for an extended source that is still much smaller than the distance that the fields travel. The prior equation was the solution for ψ(r,t) from a source S at x=y=z=0. We can rewrite this equation to make this explicit: ψ(r,t) = ∫ S(σ,t) dV / 4π|r–σ|. Here, retarded time t = t–|r–σ|/c. Setting σ = (0,0,0) restores the prior equation. Now all we have to do is extend the integral’s range V to include the entire extended source, with the proviso that |σ|<<|r|.

The equation for ψ applies to both the scalar and vector potentials, since these have wave equations of the same form. We can replace (ψ & S) with (φ & ρ/ε₀) or with any of the three components of (A & j/ε₀c²). The Lorentz Formula Feynman derives the Lorentz transformation from Maxwell’s equations, as he says Lorentz originally did. This misconception persisted until the early 20th Century. If light did require a medium, Maxwell’s equations and their calculation for the speed of light would be valid in only one inertial frame: the reference frame in which the ether is stationary. It was Einstein’s belief in the universality of Maxwell’s equations that led him to reject ether and postulate that, in all inertial frames, the speed of light is the same and Maxwell’s equations are valid. Einstein removed the roadblock that prevented scientific progress. It surprises me that Feynman repeatedly tries to minimize the importance of Einstein’s momentous contribution.

Next, we examine capacitors. The most common capacitor has two parallel conducting plates separated by a non-conducting gap. Electrical Energy in Circuits We found earlier that energy is stored in inductors and in capacitors according to these equations: inductor: U = L J² /2 capacitor: U = C V² /2

When an AC current flows through either an inductor or a capacitor, energy flows from the circuit to the device during one half-cycle, and flows from the device to the circuit during the other half-cycle. Over multiple full cycles, energy is neither gained nor lost.

Ideal inductors and capacitors are therefore called non-dissipative devices; they do not lose electrical energy to heat, radiation, or other damping effects. Like friction-less motion, these ideal devices are educational aides, not practical realities.

When an external source creates an emf in a circuit, energy is transferred between the source and the circuit. If current J flows parallel to the emf, energy is transferred from the external source to the circuit. If current J flows antiparallel to the emf, energy is transferred from the circuit to the source. Again, in ideal conditions these transfers are non-dissipative.

However, resistors are different, even ideal ones. When current J flows through a resistance R, the voltage drop across the resistor is JR, and electrical energy is lost at the rate VJ, or equivalently, J²R. The lost electrical energy is converted into heat within the body of the resistor, making it hot as a finger will immediately confirm.

Resistors are therefore dissipative devices. In an AC circuit with current flow J = J₀ exp(iωt), the average value of J² over multiples of a full cycle is J₀²/2, one-half the maximum current squared. Hence, the long-term average heat dissipation of a resistor in an AC circuit is R J₀²/2.

Now, let’s consider the electrical energy lost in an arbitrary circuit of passive elements whose impedance is Z.

As Feynman cautions in V2p22-12, we need to avoid a trap here. By now we are accustomed to analyzing periodic systems with complex functions, and taking the real part at the end. We are comfortable saying current J is the real part of J₀ exp(iωt), voltage V is the real part of V₀ exp(iωt), etc. Feynman says that works with linear equations. But with nonlinear equations, we must take care. The (real part of current J) multiplied by (real part of voltage V) is not equal to the (real part of JV). This is because JV is quadratic not linear. real {JV} = J₀ V₀ [cos²(ωt) – sin²(ωt)]

We need the former, not the latter; power equals (real current) multiplied by (real voltage).

We typically address non-ideal conditions by recognizing that each component actually has some amount of resistance, some amount of capacitance, and some amount of inductance.

Ladder Networks Ladder networks are special linear arrangements of passive devices connected in serial and parallel. They have unique characteristics and many important applications.

Like normal ladders, these networks have two long rails attached to many short rungs. Along one rail, identical circuit elements of impedance S are connected in series. The other rail is simply a long wire. Identical elements of impedance P are connected in parallel, forming the ladder’s rungs. Equal numbers of S and P devices alternate repetitively: serial, parallel, serial, parallel, … . We will call the basic repeating S-P unit a stage.

Figure 22-10 shows a ladder network in the left image that we assume continues downward to infinity. Being a passive two-terminal device, a ladder network has a specific impedance Z between its terminals at the top, which is some function of S and P. Clearly, the ladder has the same impedance Z whether the number of stages is (∞) or (∞–3). This characteristic will enable us to calculate Z.

As the middle image shows, the ladder’s overall impedance is unaffected if we cut off its bottom and replace that with a single element of impedance Z. This is because Z is the impedance of what we cut off.

In the right image, the ladder has one more stage cut off. For the ladder’s overall impedance to remain Z when one stage is removed, there must be a special relationship among S, P, and Z. That relationship is represented within the box at the lower right. The impedance of (S in series with the parallel combination of P and Z) must equal the impedance of Z alone. This means: Z = S + PZ / (P + Z)

ZP + Z² = SP + SZ + PZ Z² – ZS – SP = 0 Z = {S ± √[S² + 4SP] } / 2 Feynman rejects the negative square root. We can simplify this equation mathematically, and make it “seem more natural” as Feynman says, by redrawing the ladder. We split S in two, and redefine the repeating stage to be (S/2, P, S/2). The equation is simpler, it “seems more natural”, and it does better represent many actual ladder networks, particularly those in which S and P are continuously distributed along the network instead of being separated lumped elements.

Z is the ladder network’s characteristic impedance.

At high frequency, ω > ω₀, the impedance is entirely imaginary and energy propagates along the ladder without dissipation, as we would expect from any circuit containing only inductors and capacitors. But at low frequency, ω < ω₀, the impedance is entirely real, and the ladder acts like a resistor that dissipates energy. In V2p22-13, Feynman asks: “But how can the circuit continuously absorb energy, as a resistance does, if it is made only of inductances and capacitances? Answer: Because there is an infinite number of inductances and capacitances, so that when a source is connected to the circuit, it supplies energy to the first inductance and capacitance, then to the second, to the third, and so on. In a circuit of this kind, energy is being continually supplied to the system but it does not have time to get back out.

Energy is continually absorbed from the generator at a constant rate and flows constantly out into the network, supplying energy which is stored in the inductances and capacitances down the line. This idea suggests an interesting point about what is happening in the circuit. We would expect that if we connect a source to the front end, the effects of this source will be propagated through the network toward the infinite end. The propagation of the waves down the line is much like the radiation from an antenna which absorbs energy from its driving source; that is, we expect such a propagation to occur [but only] when the impedance is real. Since energy propagation through this type of ladder network varies with frequency, these circuits are effective filters, transmitting some electromagnetic waves but not others.

Cavity Resonators

In the prior chapter, we discussed ideal circuit elements of several types. We also said that regardless of complexity, any two-terminal electrical system S must have a linear relationship between the voltage V across its terminals and the current J flowing between them. We write this: J = (V – E) / Z

Here, E represents a voltage source within S, Z is the effective impedance of S, and each variable may be a function of frequency ω. This means any two-terminal electrical system is equivalent to a voltage source E(ω) connected in series with an impedance Z(ω).

Real Circuit Eleme Notes

In V2p23-1, Feynman views the above relationship from another angle. Assuming E(ω) and Z(ω) are precisely determined from measurements of V and J, he raises the question: “… can we [always] find a combination of our ideal elements that is equivalent to [Z(ω)]? The answer is that for any reasonable—that is, physically meaningful—function Z(ω), it is possible to approximate the situation to as high an accuracy as you wish with a circuit containing a finite set of ideal elements. We don’t want to consider the general problem now, but only look at what might be expected from physical arguments for a few cases.”

Let’s first consider a real resistor. Current flowing through a resistor, or anything else, produces a magnetic field, thus real resistors must have some amount of inductance determined by the amount of energy stored in that field. There is also a voltage drop across a resistor, thus there must be opposite charges accumulating at its ends that provide an electric field corresponding to that voltage. This means real resistors have some amount of capacitance as well.

Figure 23-1 shows an ideal resistor in the upper image, and the equivalent circuit of a real resistor in the lower image. Figure 23-1 Resistors: Ideal & Real

Engineers strive to design resistors with minimal parasitic inductance L and capacitance C. Within a circuit’s intended range of frequencies, the resistance R should dominate the resistor’s total impedance: iωL should be much less than R, and 1/iωC should be much more than R. This is easily accomplished at low frequencies. But at large enough ω, a resistor becomes a damped harmonic oscillator with a resonant frequency of 1/√LC.

Next, consider a real inductor comprised of a tightly coiled wire. Unless the wire is superconducting, it has some non-zero resistance R. This resistance is particularly important at low frequencies, where iωL is small and may become comparable to, or less than, R. At higher frequencies, opposite charges must accumulate at the coil’s ends in order to drive current against the inductor’s back emf. Adjacent windings, which have different voltages, act like the plates of a capacitor, as suggested by the small stray capacitors in the upper image of Figure 23-2.

Clearly, our capacitor has become far more “interesting” than the ideal device whose impedance is simply 1/iωC. In particular, at a sufficiently high frequency, our capacitor has a substantial inductance: it becomes an L-C circuit, which as we know can be a harmonic oscillator.

In V2p23-7, Feynman says: “If we want to treat the operation of [a capacitor] at high frequencies, we should abandon the approximations to Maxwell’s equations that we have made for treating circuits and return to the complete set of equations which describe completely the fields in space. Instead of dealing with idealized circuit elements, we have to deal with the real conductors as they are, taking into account all the fields in the spaces in between. For instance, if we want a resonant circuit at high frequencies we will not try to design one using a coil and a parallel-plate capacitor.”

Resonant Cavities

Feynman then presents a cylindrical cavity that is effectively a capacitor with a round conducting sidewall. The upper image of Figure 23-6 shows a cylindrical can of radius R, with a vertical electric field, a horizontally circulating magnet field, and opposite charges on the top and bottom plates. When E points up, B circulates counterclockwise (as viewed from above). Figure 23-6 Resonant Cavity. Figure 23-6 also shows graphs of field amplitudes vs. radius, with E in the middle image and B in the lower image. The frequency has been selected so that the electric field is zero at the sidewalls: ω=2.4048c/R. Since E is non-zero within the cavity, and nearly zero in the conducting walls, the electric field has substantial divergences at the surfaces of the upper and lower plate. This means those surfaces must have non-zero net charge (ρ=ε₀∇·E). The charge density, like the electric field, tapers to zero at the sidewalls.

Selecting E=0 at the sidewalls minimizes current flow in the sidewalls that would discharge the top and bottom plates and thereby eliminate the fields within the cavity.

Feynman says, in this mode, the oscillating electric and magnetic fields drive one another as they change, persisting even without any external connections. He emphasizes that field persistence requires E=0 at the sidewalls, which happens only when J₀(ωR/c) is zero, such as at frequency ω=2.4048c/R. The cavity is said to resonate at such frequencies.

Since ideal inductors and capacitors are non-dissipative, a pure L-C circuit could oscillate forever. But in practice, circuits that are not entirely superconducting have resistance that dissipates wave energy.

In our case, even with E=0 on the sidewalls, current must still flow. As the fields oscillate and reverse polarity, charge must flow from one plate through the sidewalls to the other plate. The fields will decline exponentially (as exp(-βt)) due to resistance in those conductors.

Energy can be fed into a cavity to compensate for losses and maintain its fields at a constant level. Figure 23-7 shows two wires inserted through small holes on opposite sides of the cavity and connected to the interior, forming small loops. Figure 23-7 Supplying Energy to Resonant Cavity. The wires are driven by opposite sides of a high-frequency voltage generator. If the generator’s frequency matches a resonant frequency of the cavity, the cavity’s fields are reinforced by fields from the changing currents in the looped wires.

Cavity Resonant Modes

In his lecture, Feynman then demonstrated how to measure a cavity’s response at selected frequencies. Again, two wires are inserted through small holes on opposite sides of a cavity and connected to its interior, forming small loops, as shown in Figure 23-8. One wire acts as a driver, and the other as a sensor. Figure 23-8 Measuring Cavity Response. The drive wire is powered by an adjustable radio frequency (RF) generator, while the sense wire is connected to a galvanometer that measures the current flow. When the RF frequency matches a resonant frequency of the cavity, the cavity resonates strongly, and a large current is induced in the sense wire that registers on the galvanometer.

Feynman used a cylindrical can with a height of 2.5 inches (6.4 cm) and a radius of 1.5 inches (3.8 cm), corresponding to a predicted resonant frequency f of: f = ω/2π = 2.4048 c/2πR = 3.014 GHz. Figure 23-9 is a stylized graph of this cavity’s sense wire current versus frequency. Figure 23-9 Cavity Response vs. Frequency.

Resonance curves are described in Feynman Simplified 1B, Chapter 13. The resonance curves here are much narrower than we can faithfully depict: for a resonant cavity with highly conductive walls, the height-to-width ratio Q can exceed 100,000.

In V2p23-9, Feynman says the actual measured resonant frequency of his can is 3.050 GHz, “…which is quite close to the predicted resonant frequency, but not exactly the same. There are several possible reasons for the discrepancy. Perhaps the resonant frequency is changed a little bit because of the holes we have cut to put in the coupling loops. A little thought, however, shows that the holes should uld lower the resonant frequency a little bit, so that cannot be the reason. Perhaps there is some slight error in the frequency calibration of the signal generator, or perhaps our measurement of the diameter of the cavity is not accurate enough. Anyway, the agreement is fairly close.”

The above is a subtle statement that good experimenters must consider all possible factors that might impact their measurements.

The reason that the holes should lower the resonant frequency is that cutting these holes is a bit like moving a small amount of wall material out to r=∞. This slightly reduces the average value of 1/R, to which the frequency is directly proportional.

Feynman also found that his can resonated at 3.30 GHz and 3.82 GHz, as indicated in Figure 23-9. While we saw that the Bessel function J (x=ωc/R) has an unlimited number of zero-crossings, Feynman says this does not explain these resonances. The second zero-crossing of J is at x=5.5201, which corresponds to f=6.92 GHz. He says we would certainly find that resonance if we increased the RF generator frequency to that level, but something else must explain the 3.30 and 3.82 resonances.

Feynman says our analysis so far was not sufficiently inclusive: we only considered a vertical electric field with a horizontally circulating magnetic field. In Feynman Simplified 1D, Chapter 44, we learned that waves confined to limited spaces can have many modes of oscillation. For example, consider a cubic cavity all of whose sides have length 2R. Our prior E-up-down solution is still a solution in a cubic cavity, but now we also have E-left-right and E-in-out solutions, all with the same resonant frequency. Feynman says: “In fact, how is the can supposed to know which is its top and bottom, and which are its sides? …If we now distort the cube into a cylinder, we will change these frequencies somewhat, [but not] too much, provided we keep the dimensions of the cavity more or less the same.”

He says the resonance at 3.30 GHz is a mode with E primarily left-right and B circulating primarily vertically. Higher order modes correspond to higher frequencies. The resonance at 3.82 GHz has the fields shown in Figure 23-10, where the solid arrows represent E and the dashed arrows represent B. Figure 23-10 Higher Resonance Mode

The apparatus in Figure 23-8 identifies resonant frequencies. We can also identify the mode of oscillation by determining the electric field direction at various points. This can be done by inserting a wire through another small hole in the cavity. When the wire is oriented parallel to E, the field drives a current in the wire, absorbing energy and depressing the galvanometer reading. This does not happen if the wire is orthogonal to E. By moving the wire from place to place and changing its orientation, the field can be mapped.

Cavities as L-C Circuits

In V2p23-10, Feynman says: “Although the resonant cavity we have been describing seems to be quite different from the ordinary resonant circuit consisting of an inductance and a capacitor, the two resonant systems are, of course, closely related. They are both members of the same family; they are just two extreme cases of electromagnetic resonators—and there are many intermediate cases between these two extremes.”

Let’s start at one “extreme”: the inductor-capacitor circuit in the upper image of Figure 23-11. For ideal components, the resonant frequency is ω=1/√LC. The resonant frequency increases if we reduce L, for example, by reducing the number of turns in the coil. That strategy reaches its limit when the coil has only one turn.

We can further reduce L and raise ω by adding inductors in parallel. The combined impedance Z of N identical inductances L connected in parallel is given by: 1/Z = N(1/L), Z = L/N. The middle image of Figure 23-11 shows a capacitor with six one-turn inductors connected in parallel. Figure 23-11 L-C Morphing Into Cavity

Continuing to add more one-turn inductors raises ω even more, and eventually results in the lower image of Figure 23-11, a capacitor that is completely enclosed on its ends. Here, the magnetic field is primarily in the loops labeled “L”, and the electric field is primarily between the middle conductors labeled “C”. Here, the central gap height is h, as before, and the height of the side loops is H.

The resonant frequency is further increased by reducing the physical size of the inductive loops. In the limit that H reduces to h, we recover the cylindrical cavity. This progression demonstrates a smooth transition from a simple L-C circuit of ideal components and fully separated fields to the opposite “extreme” of a cavity with fully integrated fields.

In V2p23-11, Feynman says that although our analysis focused on devices with cylindrical symmetry the concepts apply to conducting containers of any shape. He challenges you to calculate the modes of this device. Just kidding.

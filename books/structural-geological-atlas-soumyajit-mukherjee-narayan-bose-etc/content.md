Preface Field and thin-section studies of structures constitute a compulsory work for all the geoscience students. The purpose of bringing out this atlas is to present diverse morphological features of structures in different scales that are otherwise not possible to publish in research papers.

Incidentally, all the snaps in this book come from different Indian terrains. There has been a substantial drop of research papers in leading international journals on morphological variation of selected structural features. Also, in several teaching organizations, due to cost-cutting, ﬁeldwork has been either minimized or stopped. We understand books cannot substitute ﬁeldworks, but still, atlas books such as this can give the readers a feel of how much diverse certain structures can be morphologically. In few cases, ﬁgures have been compared, and caution in ﬁnding shear sense has been discussed in the caption. In some other cases, the reader is encouraged to interpret the images further. We have also provided some information about the terrain from where photographs/samples have been taken. Accurate structural interpretation from images can be difﬁcult based solely on ﬁeld snaps or photomicrographs.

Rather, it is much easier to interpret by observing the nearby structures in the ﬁeld and deciding whether the interpretation is repeatedly manifested by several structures over a large spatial domain. Even then posing questions from images in the classroom is a very useful exercise for the beginners. Everyone may not be able to give the most accurate answer, but their arguments can be sharpened by the instructors through (prolonged) interactions, and by drawing similar structures on the black/white board. The purpose of this atlas is not to make exhaustive review on any speciﬁc subject of structural geology, nor is to provide all possible references. The target readers for this book are the beginners in structural geology, who can develop their eyes before attempting ﬁeldworks and thin-section studies. Also, modellers will ﬁnd the book useful in understanding the subtle variation of structures.

Barmer Basin Introduction The Barmer basin in Rajasthan (India) is a narrow, failed, continental rift basin with uplifted rift shoulders exposed along its E and W margins. The basin trends NNW and comprises of thick Mesozoic and Tertiary sediments. Its tectonic evolution is somewhat linked to other Mesozoic and/or Tertiary basins of NW segment of Rajasthan, India.

The Barmer basin comprises of thick sedimentary sequence from Jurassic to recent overlying the Malani Igneous Suite.

Structural ﬁeld studies (Dasgupta and Mukherjee, 2017)

along the rift margins helped in deciphering the two-phases (NW-SE followed by NE-SW) of extension through brittle shear tectonics. Field studies further identiﬁed: (1) structural inheritance of NNW trending fractures of older Malani basement rocks in the NW trending rift faults, (2) role of transfer zones (Dasgupta and Mukherjee 2019, Dasgupta and Maitra 2018, Dasgupta 2018) in rift fault propagation.

Near NE trending mega-scale transfer zones have been documented along the Fatehgarh fault trend in N margin of the Barmer basin. See Bladon et al. (2015), Dolson et al.

(2015), Dasgupta and Mukherjee (2017), Dasgupta and Mukherjee (2019) and Sharma (2007). for recent under- standing on the tectonic of the Barmer region and its sur- roundings. Vide Passchier and Trouw (2005), Mukherjee (2011a, 2012, 2013, 2015), Mukherjee and Koyi (2010a, b)

etc. for shear sense indicators.

Lesser Himalaya Introduction The Lesser Himalayan Sequence (LHS) consists of mainly Palaeoproterozoic metapelites: slates, phyllites, psammo-pelitic schists, greywackes, etc. Both fore-thrusts (top-to-S/SW shear sense) and back-thrusts (top-to-N/NE)

have been reported from this terrain (Bose and Mukherjee 2019a, b; Banerjee et al., 2019; Mahato et al., 2019). This chapter describes mainly ﬁeld photographs and microstruc- tures from the following portions of the LHS: Almora klippe, the Ramgarh klippe, Sarahan region, Bhagirathi river section and Sikkim.

The Almora klippe consists of medium/high-grade metamorphic rocks of the Almora Group: schists, garnet-bearing mica schists and amphibolites (Singh and Patel 2017 and references therein). The Ramgarh klippe/Ramgarh thrust sheet crops out south to the Almora Klippe and brings the Palaeoproterozoic LHS rocks upon the younger LHS rocks or the Lower Miocene Siwaliks (Pearson and DeCelles 2005; Yin 2006).

Ladakh Himalaya Introduction The Ladakh Himalaya is present within the western part of the Himalayan orogenic belt. It is divided into ﬁve major zones from south to north, viz. Zanskar zone, Indus–Tsangpo suture zone (ITSZ), Ladakh Batholith, Shyok suture zone and Karakoram zone (Thanh et al. 2012; Dubey 2014). The Zanskar zone is bounded by the South Tibetan Detachment System (STDS) and the ITSZ, to the south and north, respectively. The portion of the STDS in NW India is also known as the Zanskar Shear Zone (ZSZ) (Finch et al. 2014; Kellett et al. 2018). The three major units of this zone are: Zanskar Crystalline Complex (ZCC, Precambrian), Zanskar Supergroup (ZS, Upper Proterozoic–Cretaceous–Eocene)

and the Tso Morari Crystallines (TMC, Middle–Upper Palaeozoic) (Thakur 1992; Dubey 2014). The ZCC domi- nantly comprises of gneisses and migmatites, whereas argil- lites, volcanics and carbonates make up the ZS. The TMC chieﬂy consists of meta-sedimentary units and eclogitic gneisses that are present to the south of the ITSZ (Yin 2006; Mukherjee 2011a; Mukherjee and Mulchrone 2012; Palin et al. 2017). At least, three phases of deformation have been reported for the TMC (Guillot et al. 1997; de Sigoyer et al.

2004; Epard and Steck 2008). The TMC underwent prograde metamorphism (St-Onge et al. 2013) due to subduction of the Neo-Tethys lithospheric rocks to *130-km depth beneath the Asian plate (Wilke et al. 2015), followed by UHP metamor- phism and buoyancy-driven exhumation (Beaumont et al.

2009). The Indus–Tsangpo suture zone (ITSZ) stretches for *2500 km from NW Pakistan through Ladakh and south Tibet to the Namche Barwa syntaxis and then south through Andaman Sea via Indo-Burma range, and constitutes the northern margin of the Indian plate (Ahmad et al. 2008; St-Onge et al. 2010). The stratigraphic succession of the ITSZ is bounded by the Lamayuru and Kargil Formation, at the base and the top, respectively.

Aravalli Introduction The Champaner Group, earlier designated as the Champaner Series, constitutes the youngest unit of the Aravalli Super- group. It consists of a sequence of meta-subgreywacke, sandy phyllite, graphite schist, protoquartzite, polymictic meta-conglomerate, dolomitic limestone and manganiferous phyllite (Gupta et al. 1997; Joshi and Limaye 2018; Joshi, 2019a, b) and forms the southernmost extension of the Aravalli Mountain Belt (AMB) in Gujarat. It is located at the junction between the two older sequences, viz. 1. the Lunawada Group (LG)

in the northeast and 2.

the pre-Champaner rocks in the southeast. The AMB displays a signiﬁcant progressive switch of the structural trend from NE–SW to E–W in Gujarat and continues to exhibit NW–SE trend in the older pre-Champaner rocks extending right up to parts of Madhya Pradesh. LG is the part of southernmost extension of Aravalli Mountain Belt (AMB) and exposed till northeast of Gujarat. Lithologically, the LG comprises phyllites, mica schists, calc–silicates, quartz ± chlorite schists, meta-subgreywackes, meta-siltstones, meta-semipelites, meta-protoquartzites with minor layers and thin sheets of dolomitic marbles, petromict meta-conglomerates, manganiferous phyllites and phos- phatic algal meta-dolomites (Gupta et al. 1980, 1992, 1995; Joshi et al. 2019). The Neoproterozoic rocks of the Balaram-Abu road region belong to the Basantgarh Forma- tion of the Kumbhalgarh Group, which is the part of South Delhi Fold Belt (SDFB) exposed till the northwest of Gujarat. The main rock types of the study area are pyroxene granulites forming a wedge-shaped terrain. It is bound by (i) NNE-SSW trending and NW dipping Kui-Chitrasani shear zone (KCSZ), which abut against the Erinpura Granite in the west; (ii) N–S trending “Or-Surpagla tectonic junc- tion” to northeast; and (iii) ENE–WSW trending Or-Deldar fault in the north. The regional metamorphism is character- ized by upper amphibolite to lower granulite facies condition (Desai et al. 1978; Srikarni 2000).
